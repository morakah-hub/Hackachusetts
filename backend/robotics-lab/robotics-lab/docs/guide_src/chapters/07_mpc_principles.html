Part III — Model Predictive ControlThe principle, a worked example, the project's actual torque MPC, and what its experiments show.

MPC from first principles

Model predictive control chooses each command by solving an optimization problem about the future, again and again. This chapter builds the idea from scratch, works a small example by hand and by computer, and only then turns to the seven-joint robot.

What MPC is

Intuition. When you drive, you do not decide the steering angle only from where the car is now. You look ahead at the road for the next few seconds, imagine how the car will respond to steering and braking, choose a plan that keeps you on the road comfortably, start executing its first moment, and then, a fraction of a second later, look again and re-plan. That is model predictive control:

  1. measure the current state;
  2. use a model to predict how the system would evolve over a finite future window for any candidate sequence of commands;
  3. choose the command sequence that minimizes a cost (tracking error, effort, roughness) while respecting constraints (motor limits, speed limits);
  4. apply only the first command; discard the rest; repeat at the next sampling instant.

Why it matters. PID and computed-torque control react to the current error. MPC is the standard method that anticipates the reference and handles constraints explicitly: if the motor can deliver at most 12 Nm, the optimizer plans within that limit instead of discovering it when the torque is clipped. That is exactly what distinguishes the torque MPC from CTC in this project's results.

Why it is called model predictive control

Model: it needs an explicit mathematical model of the plant's dynamics. Predictive: it uses the model to predict future states over a horizon. Control: the output is the control input applied now. The family also goes by "receding-horizon control". In industry it has been used in chemical process control since the 1970s, where slow dynamics left minutes for each optimization; fast solvers have brought it to robots with millisecond sampling times.

Receding-horizon optimization

Re-planning at every step is what makes MPC a feedback controller: each new plan starts from the measured state, so model errors and disturbances are corrected at the next update. The plan itself is open-loop over the horizon; the closed loop comes from repetition. The horizon "recedes": it always extends the same length into the future from the current time.

Prediction models: continuous to discrete time

Physics gives a continuous-time model $\dot x = f(x, u)$. The controller only changes its command at sampling instants $t_k = kT_s$ and holds it constant in between (zero-order hold). It therefore needs the discrete-time map from one sampling instant to the next:

\[ x_{k+1} = F(x_k, u_k). \]

Example: one joint. A single joint with inertia $I$ and torque $\tau$ (no gravity, no friction) has state $x = (\theta, \omega)$ and $\dot\theta = \omega$, $\dot\omega = \tau/I$. With $\tau$ constant over an interval of length $T_s$, integrating exactly gives

\[ \theta_{k+1} = \theta_k + T_s\,\omega_k + \frac{T_s^2}{2I}\,\tau_k, \qquad \omega_{k+1} = \omega_k + \frac{T_s}{I}\,\tau_k, \qquad\text{i.e.}\quad x_{k+1} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}1 & T_s\\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}}_{A} x_k + \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} T_s^2/2I \\ T_s/I\end{bmatrix}}_{B} \tau_k. \]

This is a linear model, $x_{k+1} = Ax_k + Bu_k$. Real robots are nonlinear (gravity contains sines; inertia depends on posture). Linear MPC approximates the nonlinear map by a linear (affine) one around the current operating point, $x_{k+1} \approx Ax_k + Bu_k + c$, re-computed at every update. Nonlinear MPC keeps $F$ nonlinear and solves a harder non-convex problem. The project uses linear MPC (Chapter 8).

State and control vectors

The state $x_k$ must contain everything needed to predict the future given the inputs. For a rigid arm that is the joint positions and velocities: $x = (q, \dot q) \in \mathbb{R}^{14}$ for 7 joints. The control (input) $u_k$ is what the controller sets: $u = \tau \in \mathbb{R}^7$ for the torque MPC, or 7 servo setpoints for the servo MPC. Over a horizon of $N$ steps the unknowns are $u_0, \dots, u_{N-1}$, and with them the predicted states $x_1, \dots, x_N$.

Prediction and control horizons

The prediction horizon $N$ is how many steps ahead the model looks; $N T_s$ is the look-ahead time. Some formulations use a shorter control horizon $N_u < N$, after which the input is held constant, to reduce the number of unknowns. Our MPCs use $N_u = N$. Longer horizons see further (they can start a manoeuvre earlier) but cost more computation, and a linearized model becomes less accurate far into the future. The sampling time $T_s$ trades responsiveness against the time available to solve each problem.

Values in this project

Torque MPC: $T_s$ = 20 ms (50 Hz), $N$ = 25, look-ahead 0.5 s. Servo MPC: $T_s$ = 20 ms, $N$ = 15, 0.3 s (configs/default.yaml).

Objective (cost) functions

The cost turns "good behaviour" into a number. The most common form is a sum of weighted squares:

\[ J = \sum_{k=1}^{N} (x_k - r_k)^T Q\,(x_k - r_k) \;+\; \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} u_k^T R\,u_k \;+\; \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} (u_k - u_{k-1})^T S\,(u_k - u_{k-1}). \]

The first term penalizes deviation from the reference $r_k$ (position and velocity), the second penalizes effort, the third penalizes jerky changes of the command. The diagonal weight matrices $Q$, $R$, $S$ express priorities: large $Q$ means "track tightly"; large $R$ means "save torque". Squares are used because they make the problem a convex quadratic program (below) and punish large errors much more than small ones. Often the last stage gets a larger weight (a terminal weight) to stand in for the future beyond the horizon.

State and actuator constraints

Constraints are what MPC handles better than any classical controller:

  • Input constraints $u_{\min} \le u_k \le u_{\max}$ (motor torque limits). Physically absolute, so they are imposed as hard constraints.
  • State constraints $x_{\min} \le x_k \le x_{\max}$ (joint position and speed limits). If a disturbance has already pushed the state outside, a hard state constraint makes the problem infeasible (no solution exists), which is dangerous for a controller. They are therefore usually soft: add a slack variable $s_k \ge 0$, require $x_{\min} - s_k \le x_k \le x_{\max} + s_k$, and penalize $s$ heavily in the cost, $\rho_2\|s\|^2 + \rho_1\sum s$. With a large enough linear weight $\rho_1$ (an exact penalty), the slack stays exactly zero whenever the hard version is feasible.

Quadratic programming

Collect all unknowns in one vector $z = (x_1, \dots, x_N, u_0, \dots, u_{N-1}, s_1, \dots)$. The cost is quadratic in $z$ and every constraint, including the dynamics $x_{k+1} = Ax_k + Bu_k + c$, is linear. That is a quadratic program (QP) in the standard form used by the OSQP solver:

\[ \min_z \;\tfrac12 z^T P z + q^T z \qquad \text{subject to} \qquad l \le \mathcal{A} z \le u. \]

Equality constraints are rows with $l = u$. If $P$ is positive semidefinite the problem is convex: any local minimum is the global minimum, and reliable solvers find it in a bounded, predictable number of iterations. This is the decisive practical advantage of linear MPC over nonlinear MPC.

Two ways to set up the QP exist. The condensed form eliminates the states using the dynamics ($X = \Phi x_0 + \Gamma U$), leaving only the inputs: few variables, but dense matrices. The sparse (or simultaneous) form keeps the states as variables and the dynamics as equality constraints: more variables, but very sparse matrices that a sparse solver handles efficiently. Our MPCs use the sparse form; v0.3 measured a condensed variant and found it slower (94 ms per solve) because the matrices become dense.

OSQP (Operator Splitting Quadratic Program solver) solves such QPs with ADMM, an iterative method that alternates between a linear-system solve (with a factorized KKT matrix) and a simple projection onto the bounds. Its useful properties for MPC are that a warm start from the previous solution cuts iterations, an iteration limit bounds the solve time, and solution polishing refines the approximate ADMM solution to high accuracy when the active constraints have been identified. Its tolerances eps_abs/eps_rel define "converged".

A worked example: one joint, by hand and by computer

Take the single joint above with $I$ = 0.5 kg·m² and $T_s$ = 0.1 s:

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0.1 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}, \qquad B = \begin{bmatrix}0.01 \\ 0.2\end{bmatrix}. \]

Goal: move from rest at $\theta = 0$ to $\theta = 1$ rad and stop there, so $r = (1, 0)$. Weights: $Q = \mathrm{diag}(10, 1)$, $R = 0.1$, no rate term.

Horizon N = 1, by hand

With one step, $J(u_0) = (x_1 - r)^TQ(x_1 - r) + R u_0^2$ with $x_1 = Ax_0 + Bu_0$. Setting $dJ/du_0 = 0$:

\[ 2B^TQ(Ax_0 + Bu_0 - r) + 2Ru_0 = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad u_0^* = \frac{B^TQ\,(r - Ax_0)}{B^TQB + R}. \]

From rest, $Ax_0 = 0$, $B^TQr = 0.01\cdot 10\cdot 1 = 0.1$, $B^TQB = 10\cdot 0.01^2 + 1\cdot 0.2^2 = 0.041$, so $u_0^* = 0.1/0.141 = $ 0.709 Nm. The predicted state is $x_1 = (0.0071, 0.142)$. The one-step controller is greedy: it does not "know" that velocity built up now must be braked later, except through the velocity weight.

Horizon N = 20 with a torque limit, by computer

Now look 2 s ahead ($N$ = 20) and add the hard constraint $|\tau| \le 1$ Nm. This has 60 unknowns (40 states, 20 torques), so we solve it as a QP with OSQP, exactly as the project's controllers do (docs/guide_src/make_figures.py, mpc_example).

Computed for this guide
  • Without the limit the optimal first torques are 6.43, 1.84, −0.30, −1.15, … Nm: a large kick, then braking. The first value is 6.4 times the motor's limit.
  • With the limit the optimal plan is: full torque +1.0 Nm for 6 steps (0.6 s), a partial step of 0.21 Nm, then braking (−0.71, −1.0, −1.0, −0.87, …) and a gradual approach. The predicted angle reaches 0.99 rad at the end of the horizon. This is the familiar "bang–coast–brake" shape, discovered by the optimizer rather than programmed.
  • In closed loop (re-solve every 0.1 s, apply the first torque) the joint reaches 0.78 rad at 1 s, 0.9945 rad at 2 s and 0.9999 rad at 3 s, never exceeding 1 Nm.

Notice what happened. Nothing in the controller says "accelerate, then brake". The plan emerges from three ingredients: the model (which says how torque changes the state), the cost (which says what we want), and the constraint (which says what is possible). The same three ingredients, in 14 state dimensions instead of 2, are what TorqueMPCController solves 50 times per second.

Why classical MPC needs no training

Nothing in MPC is learned from data. The model comes from physics (here, MuJoCo's equations evaluated at the current state); the cost and constraints are written by the engineer; the optimizer computes the answer from scratch at every step. There are no trainable parameters, no dataset and no training phase. Change the cost weights or the target and the very next solve reflects it. This is also why MPC needs an accurate model: it can only be as good as its predictions. (Learning can be combined with MPC, for example to learn a model or a terminal cost, but this project does not do so.)

How MPC differs from reinforcement learning and learned policies

Model predictive control versus reinforcement learning.

The two are complementary rather than rivals; many research systems put a learned high-level policy on top of a model-based low-level controller like ours (Chapter 19).

What can go wrong: solver failure, instability, missed deadlines

  • Infeasibility. Hard constraints that cannot all be satisfied (for example a state already outside a hard limit) leave the QP without a solution. Remedy: soft state constraints, which our MPCs use by default.
  • Ill-conditioning. Weights or variables spanning many orders of magnitude make the solver slow or inaccurate. The torque MPC optimizes normalized torques $\tau/\tau_{\max}$ for exactly this reason (Chapter 8).
  • Iteration limit. If OSQP needs more than max_iter = 500 iterations, the solve fails; our controllers then hand over to a fallback controller (PID or CTC) until the next successful update.
  • Model error. The plan is optimal for the model. If the model is wrong (wrong masses, linearization error far along the horizon, unmodelled push), the closed loop can perform poorly or, with aggressive weights, even become unstable: the servo MPC went unstable with a position weight of 1e5 (README, "MPC design notes").
  • Deadlines. On a real robot the next command is needed every $T_s$. If linearization plus the solve take longer, the update is late. This project measures that (deadline overruns) but, being a simulation, does not suffer from it physically (Chapter 9).
  • Stability is not automatic. Finite-horizon MPC is not guaranteed stable in general; theory provides terminal costs and constraints that guarantee it. Our controllers use a terminal weight multiplier but no terminal constraint, and their stability is established empirically, not proven.
