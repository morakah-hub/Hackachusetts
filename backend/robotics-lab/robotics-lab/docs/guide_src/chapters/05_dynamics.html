Robot dynamics and MuJoCo

Kinematics says where the robot is; dynamics says how torques make it move. This chapter derives the standard equation of motion of a robot arm and shows exactly how MuJoCo and our code represent each of its terms.

Generalized coordinates, velocities and accelerations

A rigid-body system is described by the smallest set of numbers that fixes every body's pose: its generalized coordinates. For an arm with revolute joints these are simply the joint angles $q$. Their derivatives $\dot q$ (rad/s) and $\ddot q$ (rad/s²) are the generalized velocities and accelerations. The pair $x = (q, \dot q)$ is the state: knowing it, and the torques applied from now on, determines the future motion.

Verified in source code

In MuJoCo the coordinates live in data.qpos (length nq = 9: seven arm joints, two finger slides) and the velocities in data.qvel (length nv = 9). For free bodies nq ≠ nv (quaternions), which is why the engine caches two different index arrays, arm_qpos_adr and arm_dof_adr (simulation/engine.py, lines 84–89). The controllers' state is the 14-vector $x = [q_{1..7}, \dot q_{1..7}]$ (controllers/prediction.py).

From one pendulum to the robot equation

A single joint. Take one link of mass $m$ whose centre of mass is a distance $l$ from a horizontal joint axis, with moment of inertia $I$ about the axis. With $\theta$ measured from the downward vertical, Newton's law for rotation (sum of torques = inertia × angular acceleration) gives

\[ I\ddot\theta + m g l \sin\theta = \tau, \]

where $\tau$ is the motor torque and $mgl\sin\theta$ is gravity's torque. Holding the link still at angle $\theta$ requires $\tau = mgl\sin\theta$: the gravity compensation torque.

Many joints: the Lagrangian route. For a chain, writing Newton's law for every body and eliminating the joint reaction forces is tedious. Lagrange's method is systematic. Define the kinetic energy $T(q,\dot q) = \tfrac12 \dot q^T M(q)\dot q$ (the inertia "seen" by the joints depends on the posture) and the potential energy $V(q)$ (gravity). The Euler–Lagrange equations $\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial T}{\partial \dot q} - \frac{\partial T}{\partial q} + \frac{\partial V}{\partial q} = \tau$ work out to

\[ \boxed{\; M(q)\,\ddot q + C(q,\dot q)\,\dot q + g(q) = \tau + \tau_{\text{ext}} \;} \]

with the following terms:

  • $M(q)$, the 7×7 inertia (mass) matrix: symmetric and positive definite. Its diagonal entry $M_{jj}$ is the inertia joint $j$ feels; off-diagonal entries mean that accelerating one joint pushes on others.
  • $C(q,\dot q)\dot q$, the Coriolis and centrifugal torques: the terms of $\dot M\dot q - \tfrac12 \partial(\dot q^T M \dot q)/\partial q$, quadratic in velocity. They are the "fictitious" forces of rotating frames; at the speeds in this project they are small compared with gravity and inertia.
  • $g(q) = \partial V/\partial q$, the gravity torques.
  • $\tau$, the actuator torques we command; $\tau_{\text{ext}} = J^T F$, joint torques produced by external forces $F$ (a push, a payload, contact).

Two more terms appear in a real joint and in MuJoCo: joint damping $-b\dot q$ (viscous friction) and armature $I_a$ (the reflected inertia of the motor rotor and gearbox, added to the diagonal of $M$).

Actuators: position servos versus torque motors

An actuator maps a command to a joint torque. MuJoCo's general actuator computes $\tau = \text{gain}\cdot\text{ctrl} + \text{bias}(q,\dot q)$, then clips to the force range.

  • Position servo (Menagerie default): gain $k_p$, bias $-k_p q - k_d \dot q$, so $\tau = k_p(u - q) - k_d\dot q$, clipped. $k_p$ = 4500, 4500, 3500, 3500, 2000, 2000, 2000 Nm/rad and $k_d = 0.1\,k_p$ (panda.xml, lines 265–274). The command $u$ is an angle (rad), not a torque.
  • Torque motor (our v0.3 variant): gain 1, no bias, so $\tau = u$ in Nm, clipped to ±87/±12 Nm (Panda._make_torque_actuators).

The difference matters enormously for control. A servo is itself a stiff feedback loop running inside the physics at every step; a torque motor does exactly what it is told, so all feedback must come from our controller. Chapter 6 shows the consequences.

Numerical integration

The equation of motion gives $\ddot q$; the simulator must turn that into the next state. The simplest scheme, explicit Euler, is $\dot q_{k+1} = \dot q_k + h\ddot q_k$, $q_{k+1} = q_k + h\dot q_{k}$ with step $h$. It becomes unstable for stiff systems (large damping or stiffness relative to inertia) unless $h$ is tiny. Semi-implicit Euler updates velocity first and uses the new velocity for the position, which conserves energy much better. MuJoCo's implicitfast integrator goes further: it treats the velocity-dependent forces (damping, servo $k_d$) implicitly, solving $(M + hD)\,\dot q_{k+1} = M\dot q_k + h f(q_k, \dot q_k)$ where $D$ collects the derivative of the forces with respect to velocity. Heavily damped servos stay stable at $h$ = 2 ms.

Consequence used by the code

Because damping acts implicitly, the discrete-time step is not exactly the continuous equation sampled at 2 ms. Continuous-time inverse dynamics applied to measured finite-difference accelerations leaves a residual of about $h\,b\,\ddot q$ (≈ 0.02 Nm here) even with a perfect model. The disturbance observer therefore enables mjENBL_INVDISCRETE, MuJoCo's inverse dynamics of the discrete step, on a private model copy (controllers/disturbance.py, line 54). Likewise, at the servo force-clip boundary the implicit update changes form (damping leaves it while the force is clipped), so the one-step map is discontinuous there; controllers/prediction.py handles that with a force-unlimited backup model (Chapter 8).

Contact forces and friction

When two geoms touch, a real contact produces normal force (no interpenetration) and friction (resisting sliding). MuJoCo models contacts as soft constraints: for every contact it computes a signed distance dist (negative = penetration) and solves a convex optimization for contact forces that allows a tiny, controlled penetration (parameters solref, solimp). Contacts are only generated when geoms are closer than their margin (0 by default); the planner's collision checker exploits this by setting a 20 mm margin on obstacles in its private model copy (Chapter 11). Collision detection proceeds in phases: a broadphase that discards pairs whose bounding boxes do not overlap, a midphase using bounding-volume trees within multi-geom bodies, and a narrowphase that computes the actual contact for each geom pair with a routine chosen by the pair's shape types (box-box, mesh-box via a general convex algorithm, and so on). Two of the project's most important findings concern these phases (midphase, v0.5; box-box narrowphase, v0.5.1/v0.5.2).

In our experiments contact is never wanted: success requires zero robot contact steps. Contacts appear only in the deliberately unsafe baseline, where the arm drives into the wall (1,005–1,849 contact steps; Chapter 15).

Limitations of the dynamics as simulated
  • The robot is the Menagerie model: convex-hull collision shapes, approximate inertias, no joint friction other than viscous damping, no motor dynamics, no gear backlash, no flexibility. Real Panda joints have friction and elasticity that are absent here.
  • Model-based controllers use the same model as the plant unless a mismatch experiment scales the masses and inertias (controller_model.mass_scale); only uniform mass/inertia scaling has been tested.
  • Sensors are perfect unless sensor_noise adds white Gaussian noise; there is no quantization, delay or filtering.
