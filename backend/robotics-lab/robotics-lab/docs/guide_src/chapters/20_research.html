Research readiness and future directions

An assessment of Robotics Lab as a research platform: genuine strengths, weaknesses, technical debt, valuable next experiments, and a possible v0.6 direction. Opinions in this chapter are the author's assessment based on the evidence in the previous chapters.

Genuine technical strengths

  • Measurement discipline. Metrics come from the simulated robot, not from controller predictions; failures are reported with every run; historical baselines are preserved; versions and seeds are recorded. This is rarer in student and even research code than it should be.
  • Independent verification culture. Twice, results were checked against an independent measurement and a simulator behaviour was found that invalidated a claim (midphase, box-box margins). The v0.5.2 audit used certificates rather than a second numerical solver, which is methodologically sound.
  • A strong, exact low-level controller. A torque MPC built directly on MuJoCo's discrete dynamics with hard torque limits, normalized variables, offset-free disturbance estimation and validated fallback, achieving µrad tracking in nominal conditions.
  • Clear separation between planning and control, with stated and tested assumptions (the planner is invisible to the controllers; the reference stays on checked edges).
  • Explicit, honest limitations in every report.

Engineering weaknesses

  • Exact-model optimism. Nominal results use the plant's own model; only uniform mass errors were tested.
  • Narrow scenarios. One wall family, a few waypoint sets, 5–20 seeds per condition.
  • Single-threaded Python on a desktop OS. Timing is noisy and not real-time; 100 Hz MPC does not fit.
  • No perception, no reactivity, no manipulation (Chapter 17).
  • Servo MPC remains worse than PID and has no current purpose beyond documentation.

What is standard engineering, and what could be scientifically interesting

Standard (well-known methods, carefully implemented): DLS inverse kinematics, computed-torque control, linear MPC with OSQP, disturbance observers, RRT-Connect with shortcutting, quintic and double-S time scaling, margin-based clearance. None of these is new; their value here is the integration and the measurements.

Potentially interesting, if pursued rigorously:

  • Reliability of simulator geometry queries for safety claims. The finding that a mainstream simulator's margin contacts are wrong for some primitive pairs, and the certificate-based audit, generalize: any planner that uses a physics engine's contact margins for clearance should audit them. A systematic study across engines and shape pairs would be a useful contribution.
  • Certified clearance under tracking error. Combining the discrete edge certificate with measured or bounded tracking error of the torque MPC to state an execution-level clearance bound.
  • When does torque MPC beat CTC + observer? The data show MPC wins nominal tracking and constraint handling while CTC + observer wins disturbance rejection; characterizing the trade-off (noise, delay, model error) is a well-posed controlled study.
  • Perception-in-the-loop margins: how clearance must grow with depth-estimation error (Chapter 18's experiment).

The most valuable next experiments

  1. Realistic sensing and delay: encoder quantization, velocity filtering, one control period of computation delay; re-tune and compare MPC and CTC + observer.
  2. Richer model error: friction, damping, armature, centre of mass, payload; test the observer's limits.
  3. Randomized planning scenarios (random obstacles, starts and goals) with hundreds of episodes, to estimate failure rates below the few percent the current samples can bound.
  4. Blended trajectories through via points (with the blended curve collision-checked), measuring time saved.
  5. LTV/SQP-RTI prediction to reduce the k³ growth of open-loop prediction error, and its effect on constraint predictions.

Connecting to autonomous-manipulation research

Most current manipulation research combines learned components (perception, grasp prediction, policies trained by imitation or reinforcement learning) with model-based layers for safety and precision. Robotics Lab's motion layer is a reasonable model-based bottom layer: a learned policy could output goals or short reference segments, the planner and collision checker would act as a safety filter, and the torque MPC would execute. The evaluation harness (seeded episodes, exact clearance, contact counts, explicit failure statuses) is directly reusable for evaluating such policies. The prerequisite is a manipulation task (objects, grasping) and, for learning, many fast simulated episodes, which raises the cost of the current Python MPC.

An appropriate v0.6 direction

Proposal — not started

The project's own plan names pick-and-place next. A focused v0.6 could be a minimal, measured pick-and-place in a known world:

  1. add one graspable box to a scene and a stable placement target;
  2. extend IK to full 6-D pose (position + orientation) for top-down grasps;
  3. phase-dependent collision rules (allow finger–object contact during grasp; treat the grasped object as robot geometry while carrying);
  4. close the gripper through actuator 8 and verify grasp success physically (object lifts, does not slip);
  5. carry with the disturbance observer absorbing the payload; measure tracking with and without the observer;
  6. evaluate over seeded object poses: success rate, contacts other than the grasp, clearance, placement error.

This uses every existing layer, adds the missing manipulation pieces one at a time, and keeps the project's discipline of measured, reproducible claims. Perception and reactive replanning would naturally follow as v0.7+.
