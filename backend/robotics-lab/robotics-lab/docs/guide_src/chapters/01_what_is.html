What is Robotics Lab?

A first look: the robot, the simulator, the problems the project tries to solve, and the vocabulary used in the rest of the guide.

Why this project exists

Robotics Lab is a simulation platform for a robot arm. It contains no hardware and no real robot. A computer model of a seven-joint industrial research arm, the Franka Emika Panda, is simulated by the physics engine MuJoCo, and our Python code decides what torques the arm's motors apply at every instant. The goal is to build, piece by piece and with measured evidence, the software stack a real autonomous manipulator needs:

  1. Move accurately. Given a desired joint trajectory, make the simulated arm follow it with small error while respecting motor torque limits and joint speed limits (control).
  2. Move to places, not just joint angles. Given a desired hand position in space, find joint angles that put the hand there (inverse kinematics).
  3. Avoid obstacles. When the obvious motion would hit something, find a different collision-free route with a safety margin (motion planning and collision checking).
  4. Be honest about it. Measure every claim with reproducible experiments and automated tests, and record failures and limitations alongside successes.

The fourth point shapes the whole repository. Most of its 7,000 lines of project Python are the controller, planner and simulation code. A large share, however, consists of benchmarks, audit tools and 189 automated tests, and every milestone has a written research report in docs/research/.

Simulation comes first because it is cheap, repeatable and safe. A controller bug that drives a real Panda into a table can damage an expensive robot; in MuJoCo it just produces a number in a CSV file. Simulation also gives perfect measurements (the exact joint angles, the exact distance to an obstacle), which makes it possible to test whether an algorithm is correct before asking whether it is robust to sensor noise. The price is the reality gap: anything learned in simulation needs to be checked again on hardware. This project has not crossed that gap (Chapter 17).

The robot: a Franka Emika Panda

The Panda is a collaborative robot arm widely used in research labs. It has seven revolute (rotating) joints in a chain from the base to the hand, plus a two-finger parallel gripper. Seven joints give it one more degree of freedom than the six needed to place a hand at an arbitrary position and orientation in 3-D space, so it is redundant: the same hand position can be reached with a whole family of arm postures (elbow up, elbow down and everything between).

Verified in source code

The arm model is a verbatim copy of Google DeepMind's MuJoCo Menagerie Panda (robots/models/franka_emika_panda/, commit d7b481b recorded in SOURCE.txt). It is never edited; every change (torque motors, an end-effector site) is made in code by robots/panda.py. The arm's total mass in the model is 17.45 kg. Joint limits come from the XML; speed, acceleration and jerk limits from the Franka datasheet are constants in robots/panda.py (VELOCITY_LIMITS = 2.175 rad/s for joints 1–4 and 2.61 rad/s for joints 5–7). The motor torque limits are 87 Nm (joints 1–4) and 12 Nm (joints 5–7).

The Menagerie model ships with position servos: you command a desired joint angle and a stiff spring-damper inside the simulator pulls the joint there. In v0.3 the project added a torque-motor variant in which our code commands the joint torques directly, which is how modern research controllers are usually written. Both variants exist side by side (Panda(actuation="position") or "torque"), and the choice of controller selects the variant automatically (Chapter 3).

The environment and the physics engine

MuJoCo ("Multi-Joint dynamics with Contact") is an open-source physics engine designed for robotics and control research. Given the robot's current state (joint angles and velocities) and the motor commands, it computes how the state changes over one small time step, including gravity, inertia, joint friction-like damping, motor limits and contact forces when bodies touch. Calling it repeatedly advances simulated time. In this project one physics step is 2 ms (500 steps per simulated second), with MuJoCo's implicitfast integrator.

An environment is a MuJoCo scene file in environments/: a floor, usually a table, sometimes obstacles. The robot is attached at runtime to a site named robot_mount, so scene files know nothing about robots. There are five scenes: empty, table, obstacles (a box and a cylinder on the table), planning_demo (a wall across the table) and planning_narrow (the wall plus a ceiling, leaving a 34 cm gap).

What the software currently does

As of v0.5.2 the software can, entirely in simulation:

  • drive the arm through a list of joint-angle waypoints, or to a list of hand positions, with four interchangeable controllers: a PID outer loop on the position servos, a servo-setpoint model predictive controller (MPC), computed-torque control (CTC) and a torque-level MPC;
  • generate smooth joint trajectories whose timing is automatically stretched until they respect the velocity, acceleration, jerk and torque limits;
  • estimate unknown disturbance torques (pushes, wrong masses) and compensate them;
  • plan collision-free motions around known obstacles with a sampling-based planner (RRT-Connect), keeping 20 mm of clearance at every checked configuration, and shorten the resulting paths;
  • replan when the obstacle geometry is different at the start of an episode;
  • measure all of this in benchmarks that report tracking error, constraint violations, contacts, clearances and solver timing.

It does not perceive the world (no cameras), does not replan during motion, does not grasp objects, has no learning component, and has never controlled a real robot. Chapter 17 gives a precise capability matrix.

Simulation, planning, control, perception, learning

These five words are used loosely in everyday speech; in robotics they name distinct layers with different inputs, outputs and time scales. Keeping them apart is the single most useful habit for understanding this project.

The layers of a robot software stack and where each stands in Robotics Lab.
LayerQuestion it answersTypical rateIn this project
SimulationGiven motor commands, how does the world move?500 Hz here (2 ms)MuJoCo, wrapped by simulation/engine.py. Replaces the real world.
PerceptionWhat is around the robot, and where?10–60 Hz (cameras)Absent. Obstacle geometry is read from the scene file: the planner is given the truth.
PlanningWhich route through space reaches the goal without collision?once per task hereplanning/: IK postures, RRT-Connect, shortcutting, collision checking. Runs once before motion starts.
Trajectory generationHow fast should the robot move along that route?once per tasktasks/trajectory.py: time-scaled minimum-jerk or double-S profiles.
ControlWhich motor commands make the arm follow the trajectory now?50–500 Hzcontrollers/: PID, servo MPC, CTC, torque MPC.
LearningHow can behaviour improve from data?offlineAbsent. No trained model anywhere in the code.

A frequent confusion is to expect the controller to avoid obstacles. In this design it cannot: the controllers receive only a joint reference and the measured joint state, and know nothing about walls. Obstacle avoidance happens before motion, in the planner, and the controllers keep the arm on the planned route. Chapter 9 explains why closed-loop MPC alone does not give obstacle avoidance.

What is autonomous and what is predefined

"Autonomous" means that the robot itself chooses actions to achieve a goal. Here is what the robot decides and what a human decided in advance:

Decisions made by the software versus decisions fixed by a person.
Decided by the software at run timeFixed in advance by a person
Joint angles that reach a requested hand position (IK, with several candidate postures)The goal positions or joint waypoints themselves (YAML config)
A collision-free route around known obstacles (random-sampling planner)The obstacle geometry (scene files, optional overrides)
The duration of each motion segment, to respect limitsThe limits and safety fractions (YAML)
Every motor torque, 50–500 times per second, from measured stateController type, gains, weights, horizons (YAML)
Rejecting an unreachable or blocked goal, with a reasonWhat to do next after a rejection (nothing: the target is skipped)

So the robot is autonomous at the level of motion (how to get there safely) but not at the level of tasks (what to do and why). There is no perception, no task planner and no decision-making beyond "reach the listed targets in order".

The long-term vision

The project's own documents state the direction: next pick-and-place, then reinforcement learning and other learning methods (CLAUDE.md, "Planned next: pick-and-place. RL and learning come later"). The pieces built so far (accurate torque control, feasibility-aware trajectories, collision-aware planning with clearance, and a careful evaluation harness) are the foundation such work needs. Chapters 18–20 discuss what would have to be added: perception, world models, reactive replanning, grasping, and how learned components could plug into the existing interfaces.
