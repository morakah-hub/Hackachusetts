Control theory fundamentals

How to compute motor commands that make the arm follow a reference: feedback, stability, PID, computed-torque control, disturbance observers, and the difference between position and torque control. Every controller in the repository is explained and compared.

Open loop, closed loop and feedback

Open-loop control computes commands from the goal alone, without looking at what the robot actually does: "apply this torque profile for 3 s". If the model is slightly wrong, or someone pushes the arm, the error grows unchecked. Closed-loop (feedback) control measures the actual state and corrects: the command depends on the tracking error $e = q_{\text{ref}} - q$. Every controller in Robotics Lab is closed-loop: compute() receives the measured $q$ and $\dot q$ at every 2 ms step.

Stability and the second-order error equation

A controller is stable if errors die out instead of growing. The workhorse model is the damped second-order system

\[ \ddot e + 2\zeta\omega_n \dot e + \omega_n^2 e = 0. \]

Its solutions behave like $e^{\lambda t}$ with $\lambda = -\zeta\omega_n \pm \omega_n\sqrt{\zeta^2 - 1}$. If both roots have negative real part the error decays. $\omega_n$ (rad/s) sets how fast the error decays and $\zeta$ sets how much it oscillates: $\zeta = 1$ ("critical damping") is the fastest decay without overshoot. The settling time to about 2% is roughly $4/(\zeta\omega_n)$. For discrete-time systems $x_{k+1} = A x_k$, the condition becomes: all eigenvalues of $A$ inside the unit circle, $|\lambda_i| < 1$.

Example from our model

The torque MPC's 20 ms prediction model, linearized at the home pose with the gravity-holding torque held constant, has a largest eigenvalue magnitude of 1.09 (computed for this guide). That is greater than 1, so holding a constant torque is open-loop unstable at that pose: any small deviation grows, as for a balanced pendulum. This is why the arm cannot be run open-loop and why the MPC must re-plan from fresh measurements.

PID control

Intuition. Push against the error (P), push harder if the error persists (I), and brake if the error is changing fast (D):

\[ u = K_p e + K_i \int_0^t e\,dt' + K_d \dot e. \]

P alone acts like a spring: it oscillates, and under a constant load such as gravity it leaves a steady error, since some error is needed to produce the holding force. D adds damping. I accumulates the remaining error until the holding force is supplied, at the cost of slower, possibly oscillatory transients and integrator windup if the actuator saturates. shows all four behaviours on a toy joint.

Verified in source code: JointPIDController (v0.1)

controllers/pid.py. It does not command torques. It shifts the setpoint of the Menagerie position servos, which are themselves PD controllers inside MuJoCo:

\[ u = q_{\text{ref}} + k_p e + k_i\!\int e\,dt + k_d(\dot q_{\text{ref}} - \dot q) + k_{\text{ff}}\,\dot q_{\text{ref}}, \]

with $k_p$ = 0.5, $k_i$ = 5.0, $k_d$ = 0, $k_{\text{ff}}$ = 0.1 (configs/default.yaml). The servo produces $\tau = k_{p,s}(u - q) - k_{d,s}\dot q$. Without the outer loop, gravity makes the servo sag about 0.008 rad. The integral term supplies the holding offset. The feedforward term $0.1\,\dot q_{\text{ref}}$ cancels the servo's damping lag, because for every Panda servo $k_{d,s}/k_{p,s} = 0.1$, so a joint moving at $\dot q_{\text{ref}}$ needs its setpoint $0.1\,\dot q_{\text{ref}}$ ahead. Anti-windup clamps the integral's contribution to 0.05 rad. Runs every 2 ms. Result (CLAUDE.md, tests): maximum tracking error below 4 mrad, steady-state error around 0.1 mrad.

Feedforward and feedback

Feedback reacts to errors after they appear; feedforward supplies the command the reference is known to need, before any error appears. If you know the motion will require 20 Nm to hold against gravity and 5 Nm to accelerate, apply 25 Nm directly and let feedback handle only what the model got wrong. Good controllers combine both. In the project: PID's $k_{\text{ff}}\dot q_{\text{ref}}$, CTC's inverse dynamics of $\ddot q_{\text{ref}}$, and MPC's reference preview (it sees the whole next 0.5 s of the reference) are all feedforward.

Computed-torque control

Intuition. If we know the robot's dynamics exactly, we can compute the torque that produces any desired acceleration, effectively cancelling gravity, inertia coupling and Coriolis forces. What remains is a simple double integrator per joint, which a PD law stabilizes.

Derivation. Choose the desired acceleration from the reference acceleration plus PD feedback, $\ddot q_{\text{des}} = \ddot q_{\text{ref}} + K_p e + K_d \dot e$, and the torque from inverse dynamics, $\tau = M(q)\ddot q_{\text{des}} + C(q,\dot q)\dot q + g(q)$. Substituting into the robot equation (with $\tau_{\text{ext}} = 0$) gives $M(\ddot q - \ddot q_{\text{des}}) = 0$, and since $M$ is invertible, $\ddot q = \ddot q_{\text{des}}$, i.e.

\[ \ddot e + K_d \dot e + K_p e = 0. \]

With $K_p = \omega_n^2$ and $K_d = 2\zeta\omega_n$ this is the standard second-order error equation: the gains directly set the error dynamics, identically for every posture.

Verified in source code: ComputedTorqueController (v0.3)

controllers/computed_torque.py. $\omega_n$ = 25 rad/s, $\zeta$ = 1, so $K_p$ = 625 s⁻², $K_d$ = 50 s⁻¹. The torque is ArmInverseDynamics.torque(q, qd, qdd_des), i.e. MuJoCo's mj_inverse, which includes armature and joint damping, clipped to the torque limits. Runs every 2 ms. It uses the controller's model (the plant itself unless mismatch is configured), so in nominal runs its model is perfect. It ignores velocity and joint limits: it follows any reference, feasible or not. v0.4 options: integral action $\ddot q_{\text{des}} \mathrel{+}= k_i\int e$ (error dynamics $\dddot e + K_d\ddot e + K_p\dot e + k_i e = 0$, stable by the Routh criterion only if $k_i < K_dK_p$ = 31,250, enforced by the constructor) and a disturbance observer ($\tau \leftarrow \tau - \hat d$). It is also the torque MPC's fallback.

Disturbance estimation

Intuition. A model-based controller is only as good as its model. Unknown forces, such as a push, a payload or masses that are 15% wrong, act like an unknown extra torque $d$. If the controller could measure $d$ it could cancel it. It cannot measure it directly, but it can infer it: compare the acceleration the robot actually showed with the acceleration the model predicted for the torque that was applied.

Mathematics. Model the plant as $M(q)\ddot q + h(q,\dot q) = \tau_{\text{applied}} + d$. Each physics step, using the discrete inverse dynamics ID:

\[ d_{\text{raw},k} = \mathrm{ID}\!\left(q_{k-1}, \dot q_{k-1}, \frac{\dot q_k - \dot q_{k-1}}{\Delta t}\right) - \tau_{\text{applied},k-1}, \qquad \hat d_k = \hat d_{k-1} + \frac{\Delta t}{T}\left(d_{\text{raw},k} - \hat d_{k-1}\right). \]

The second equation is a first-order low-pass filter with time constant $T$ that smooths the noisy raw estimate. Using the applied (post-clipping) torque means the estimate does not wind up when the motors saturate.

Verified in source code: JointDisturbanceObserver (v0.4)

controllers/disturbance.py: exactly the two equations above, with mjENBL_INVDISCRETE on a private model copy, $T$ = 10 ms, and $|\hat d|$ clipped to half the torque limit. The controller calls update(q, qd, dt) each step and applied(tau) after choosing its command. Off by default for CTC, on by default for the torque MPC, where it turns the MPC "offset-free" (Chapter 8).

Reported result (v0.4 report §5)

Under a 30 N sideways push on the hand for 1 s, maximum joint error: CTC 35.2 mrad → 3.14 mrad with the observer; torque MPC 12.1 → 3.62 mrad. With controller masses 25% too low: CTC 9.09 → 0.84 mrad; MPC 3.44 → 0.95 mrad. Observer time constant chosen from a noise/rejection trade-off. With sensor noise, step-to-step torque jitter for CTC was 0.21 Nm without observer, 0.45 Nm at T = 10 ms and 0.78 Nm at T = 5 ms (which rejected the push to 1.6 mrad).

Wording discrepancy: CLAUDE.md says a 5 ms observer "triples torque jitter". The report's table shows ×1.7 relative to the chosen 10 ms observer and ×3.7 relative to no observer; the numbers above are the report's.

Position control versus torque control

With position servos, a stiff PD loop (up to 4500 Nm/rad) inside the simulator holds each joint; our controller only moves the setpoints. This is robust against pushes (the servo resists immediately) and needs no model, but the controller has only indirect authority over torque. Each change of setpoint $\Delta u$ creates a torque spike $k_p\Delta u$ lasting about 1 ms, which is clipped at the 12 Nm wrist limit. With torque motors, the controller decides every Nm. It can respect torque limits exactly and exploit the dynamics, but it is responsible for everything, including holding the arm against gravity. If its model is wrong or it has no integral/observer action, it deflects under pushes.

Reported result (v0.3 report §8, finding 3)

Under the 30 N push, the servo controllers deflected 3.4 mrad (PID) and 2.0 mrad (servo MPC). The torque controllers without observer deflected 12 mrad (MPC) and 35 mrad (CTC). Stiff servos reject unmodelled pushes better than torque control without disturbance estimation.
