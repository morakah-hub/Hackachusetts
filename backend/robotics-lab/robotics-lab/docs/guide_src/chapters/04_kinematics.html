Robot mechanics and kinematics

Kinematics is the geometry of motion without forces: given the joint angles, where is the hand? Given a desired hand position, which joint angles reach it? How do small joint motions move the hand?

Joints and degrees of freedom

Intuition. Your arm is a chain of rigid bones connected by joints. Each joint allows one kind of relative motion. A door hinge allows rotation about one axis: one degree of freedom (DOF). A drawer slide allows translation along one axis: also one DOF. A robot arm is a chain of such joints, and the number of independent joint motions is its number of DOF.

Why it matters. A rigid object in space has 6 DOF (3 for position, 3 for orientation). To place a tool anywhere with any orientation, an arm needs at least 6 joints. The Panda has 7 revolute (hinge) joints, so for any reachable hand pose there is (generically) a one-parameter family of arm configurations; if only the hand position is specified, as in our IK, the family has 7 − 3 = 4 dimensions. This redundancy is useful (the elbow can move away from an obstacle while the hand stays put) and is exploited by the planner's multiple IK postures (Chapter 10).

Coordinate frames and rotation matrices

Intuition. To say where something is, you need a reference: an origin and three perpendicular axes. Robotics attaches such a frame to the world and one to every link. The pose of a link is the position and orientation of its frame relative to its parent's frame.

Mathematics. Orientation is described by a rotation matrix $R \in \mathbb{R}^{3\times3}$ whose columns are the link frame's $x$, $y$, $z$ axes written in the parent frame. A rotation by angle $\theta$ about the $z$ axis is

\[ R_z(\theta) = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta & 0 \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Rotation matrices are orthonormal: $R^T R = I$ and $\det R = 1$, so $R^{-1} = R^T$. A vector $p_B$ expressed in frame B is expressed in frame A as $p_A = R_{AB}\, p_B$. Composing rotations is matrix multiplication: $R_{AC} = R_{AB} R_{BC}$, and the order matters. MuJoCo stores orientations internally as unit quaternions $(w, x, y, z)$ (four numbers, no singularities) and also provides rotation matrices (data.xmat, data.site_xmat).

The angle between two orientations $R_a$ and $R_b$ is the angle of the relative rotation $R_a^T R_b$. The project computes it in rotation_angle (simulation/kinematics.py, line 30) as $2\,\mathrm{atan2}(\|v\|, |w|)$ from the quaternion $(w, v)$ of $R_a^T R_b$, which is numerically accurate near 0 and π where the textbook formula $\arccos\big((\mathrm{tr}(R_a^TR_b)-1)/2\big)$ loses precision. It is used for the orientation metrics of the benchmark (v0.4).

Homogeneous transformations

A pose has a rotation and a translation. The homogeneous transformation packs both into one 4×4 matrix so that composing poses becomes a single multiplication:

\[ T_{AB} = \begin{bmatrix} R_{AB} & p_{AB} \\ 0\;0\;0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \begin{bmatrix} p_A \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = T_{AB} \begin{bmatrix} p_B \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad T_{AC} = T_{AB} T_{BC}. \]

Here $p_{AB}$ is the position of B's origin in A. For a revolute joint with angle $q_i$ about the link's local $z$ axis, the transform from link $i-1$ to link $i$ is a fixed offset (how the links are bolted together) followed by the joint rotation: $T_{i-1,i}(q_i) = T^{\text{fixed}}_{i}\; \mathrm{Rot}_z(q_i)$.

Forward kinematics

Intuition. Forward kinematics (FK) answers: if I set the joints to $q$, where is the hand? Walk down the chain from the base, applying each link's fixed offset and joint rotation in turn.

\[ T_{0,\text{EE}}(q) = T^{\text{fixed}}_1 R_z(q_1)\; T^{\text{fixed}}_2 R_z(q_2)\cdots T^{\text{fixed}}_7 R_z(q_7)\; T_{7,\text{EE}}. \]

A planar example. Two links of lengths $L_1$, $L_2$ moving in a plane with angles $\theta_1$ (from the $x$ axis) and $\theta_2$ (relative to link 1):

\[ x = L_1\cos\theta_1 + L_2\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2), \qquad y = L_1\sin\theta_1 + L_2\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2). \]

The map from angles to position is nonlinear (sines and cosines) but easy to evaluate.

In our code. We never write these matrices out by hand. MuJoCo's mj_kinematics walks the body tree defined in the XML and computes every body, geom and site pose. ArmKinematics.fk(q) (simulation/kinematics.py, line 79) copies the live state into a private MjData, overwrites the seven arm joints with $q$, calls mj_kinematics, and returns the position of ee_site; fk_pose also returns its rotation matrix. The engine has an equivalent forward_kinematics. Because they use scratch data, FK never disturbs the running simulation. At the home configuration $q = (0, 0, 0, -\pi/2, 0, \pi/2, -\pi/4)$ the end-effector site is at (0.554, 0, 0.921) m in the world (computed for this guide).

Jacobians

Intuition. If I nudge the joints by small amounts $\delta q$, how does the hand move? For small motions the answer is linear: $\delta x \approx J(q)\,\delta q$. The matrix $J$ is the Jacobian; its column $j$ is the hand velocity produced by moving joint $j$ alone at 1 rad/s.

Mathematics. Differentiate FK with respect to time: $\dot x = \frac{\partial x}{\partial q}\dot q = J(q)\dot q$. For the planar arm,

\[ J = \begin{bmatrix} -L_1 \sin\theta_1 - L_2\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2) & -L_2 \sin(\theta_1+\theta_2) \\ \phantom{-}L_1 \cos\theta_1 + L_2\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2) & \phantom{-}L_2\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2) \end{bmatrix}. \]

For a revolute joint $j$ with unit axis $a_j$ through point $o_j$, the geometric rule is: column $j$ of the position Jacobian is $a_j \times (p_{\text{EE}} - o_j)$. Its length is at most the distance from the joint axis to the hand. The same cross-product bound reappears in Chapter 11, where it limits how far any point of the robot can move between collision checks.

The Jacobian also maps forces: a force $F$ at the hand requires joint torques $\tau = J^T F$ (by the principle of virtual work, since power $F\cdot\dot x = F^T J \dot q = \tau^T\dot q$). This is how the disturbance experiments' 30 N push at the hand turns into joint torques.

In our code. ArmKinematics.jacobian(q) calls MuJoCo's mj_jacSite for the end-effector site and keeps the 3×7 positional block for the arm joints. test_fk_matches_engine_and_jacobian_matches_finite_differences checks it against finite differences of FK.

Inverse kinematics

Intuition. Inverse kinematics (IK) answers the reverse question: which joint angles put the hand at a desired position $x^*$? There is no general closed-form answer for the Panda, there may be no solution (target too far) or infinitely many (redundancy). The standard approach is iterative: start somewhere, measure the error $e = x^* - \mathrm{FK}(q)$, take a joint step that reduces it, repeat.

Mathematics: damped least squares. We want $\delta q$ with $J\delta q \approx e$. With 7 unknowns and 3 equations there are many solutions; the minimum-norm one is $\delta q = J^+ e$ with the pseudoinverse $J^+ = J^T(JJ^T)^{-1}$. Near singular configurations $JJ^T$ is nearly singular and the step explodes. Damped least squares (DLS) instead minimizes $\|J\delta q - e\|^2 + \lambda^2\|\delta q\|^2$, whose solution is

\[ \delta q = J^T (J J^T + \lambda^2 I)^{-1} e. \]

The damping $\lambda$ trades accuracy for robustness: steps stay bounded even at singularities. Because the arm is redundant, any joint motion in the null space of $J$ (motions that do not move the hand) can be added freely. Our solver uses it to pull the posture towards a rest configuration $q_{\text{rest}}$:

\[ \delta q \mathrel{+}= k_{\text{null}}\,(I - J^+J)(q_{\text{rest}} - q). \]

Each step is limited to 0.2 rad per joint and $q$ is clamped to the joint limits minus a margin.

Verified in source code

ArmKinematics.solve_ik(target, seed, tol=1e-4, max_iter=300, rest=None) (simulation/kinematics.py, lines 118–141) implements exactly the two equations above with $\lambda$ = 0.05 (config ik.damping), $k_{\text{null}}$ = 0.1, step limit 0.2 rad, joint-limit margin 0.05 rad, and converges when the position error is below 0.1 mm (configurable ik.tolerance). Orientation is left free. It returns an IKResult(q, success, error, iterations, reason). plan_target (v0.3) tries the current configuration and then the rest posture as seeds and checks collisions; planning/ik.py (v0.5) tries 22 seeds and keeps several valid postures; for perturbed and random seeds it passes rest=seed so the null-space term does not drag every solution to the same posture.

In configs/reach.yaml, the target out_of_reach at (1.30, 0, 0.60) m is about 1.3 m from the base; IK cannot converge and the task reports "IK did not converge (closest 360.6 mm)". Joint-space versus Cartesian-space motion

A motion can be straight in joint space (every joint moves linearly from start to goal, all arriving together) or straight in Cartesian space (the hand moves along a line). They are different. A straight line in joint space generally produces a curved hand path, because FK is nonlinear. All motions in this project are straight in joint space: tasks/trajectory.py defines each move as $q(t) = q_a + (q_b - q_a)\,s(t)$. This choice has three advantages: joint limits are respected automatically (the joint-limit box is convex, so a line between two points inside it stays inside), timing limits per joint are easy to apply, and collision checking can bound the motion of every robot point (Chapter 11). The cost is that the hand path is not a straight line; the planned paths drawn in the viewer (planning/visualization.py, ee_polyline) show these curves.

Singularities and workspace limits

A configuration is singular when $J$ loses rank: some hand direction cannot be produced at all (for example when the arm is fully stretched, it cannot move the hand further outwards). Near a singularity small hand motions need very large joint velocities. The singular values of $J$ measure how close the arm is: the smallest one is the hand speed (m/s) achievable per rad/s of joint speed in the worst direction. Computed for this guide with ArmKinematics.jacobian: at home the singular values are 0.81, 0.73 and 0.31 m/rad; at the wall demo's start 0.78, 0.73 and 0.30 m/rad, so both are comfortably non-singular.

The workspace is the set of reachable hand positions. For the Panda it is roughly a sphere of radius about 0.85 m around the shoulder, minus regions excluded by joint limits and self-collision. Reachability is not checked analytically anywhere in the code; a target is unreachable when IK fails from all seeds.

Limitations
  • IK is position-only: the hand's orientation is whatever the solver ends at (the benchmark reports the hand turning by up to 49° in the reach scenario, docs/research/v0.4-results.md §6). Grasping would need orientation control.
  • IK failure from all seeds does not prove a target is unreachable; it is a numerical search.
  • There is no Cartesian-space motion (straight-line hand paths) and no velocity-level Cartesian control.
